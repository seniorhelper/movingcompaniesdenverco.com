Chapter 1

Verify a Denver mover in ten minutes

A numbered checklist you can run from your phone while the company is still on the line.

Nothing here needs special skills. You need a phone, a browser and the company's full legal name. Work through the list in order; if a step fails, you have your answer and can stop.

  1. Get the exact name, address and number1 minAsk the company for its legal business name, street address and the permit or registration number it operates under. A company that dodges this has already told you something. In Colorado the mover's permit number must be printed on its advertisements and on every contract, so you should be able to find it without asking.
  2. Decide which rulebook applies30 secStaying inside Colorado means the state PUC regulates the mover. Crossing a state line means the federal Department of Transportation does. Chapter 2 explains the edge cases, such as a Denver-to-Boulder move compared with Denver-to-Cheyenne.
  3. Local: run the PUC permit search2 minThe PUC publishes a permit search for exactly this purpose. Look for an active household goods permit that matches the name and number you were given. The number begins with the letters HHG. Not found, expired or a different company name: treat it as a fail until the PUC says otherwise. You can also call the PUC at 303-894-2070.
  4. Interstate: look up the USDOT number2 minOn the federal Protect Your Move pages, search the company by USDOT number or name and confirm it is authorized to move household goods across state lines and that its status is active.
  5. Ask about insurance, and ask for proof1 minA legitimate mover can email a certificate of insurance on request. Building managers often ask for one too, so you will want it anyway (see Chapter 6). The PUC rules set the insurance minimums for permitted movers; we point you to them rather than quote figures we have not been able to confirm line by line.
  6. Check the Better Business Bureau record1 minOur own bar is an A+ rating, but read past the letter. The grade is a summary; the complaint text and how the company answered each one tell you more than the letter does.
  7. Ask for an in-person or video survey1 minThe PUC recommends requesting an in-person inspection of your belongings, because that is the best way to make the estimate accurate. A price given over the phone for an unseen apartment is a guess.
  8. Demand the written estimate1 minIt must be written, list the total costs and say what they are based on. In Colorado it is due to you at least 24 hours before the move unless you booked with less than 24 hours of notice. Chapter 3 has the line-by-line read.
  9. Read the contract before any depositvariesUnder Colorado law it has to carry the permit statement and number, an itemized breakdown of costs, the forms of payment accepted and a written consumer advisement. If a single item is missing, ask for a corrected copy.
  10. Compare at least two moreyour callOne quote tells you nothing. Three comparable, written, itemized estimates tell you what the move costs and who is an outlier in either direction.

Where the ten minutes go. Steps 3 or 4 are the ones that matter most. Everything else refines your choice; those two decide whether the company is allowed to move you at all.

If the check fails

A missing permit does not always mean fraud. A name mismatch can be a trade name or a typo. But it is the mover's job to clear it up before you pay anything, not yours to hope. The PUC warns that using an unpermitted mover can lead to theft or uninsured damage to your property.
